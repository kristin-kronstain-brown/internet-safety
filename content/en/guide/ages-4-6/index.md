## Focus

This is the right age to discuss:
- Privacy
- Asking before clicking or sharing
- Trusted adults
- Online people are real people, but sometimes pretending


## Lesson 1: Privacy

Start with what "private" means, because a 4 year old has no reason to know that a birthday is worth anything to anyone. Teach that some information is private:
- full name
- age
- birthday
- address
- school
- phone number
- passwords, device codes
- photos
- location
- social media accounts

Simple phrase: "Private information stays with trusted grown-ups."

There is also a [word search](private-information-word-search.png) for this lesson.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Private or OK to share?

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 10 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the twelve cards. Make two piles on the table, one labeled **Private** and one labeled **OK to share**. Read each card out loud and let your kid choose the pile. Do not correct a wrong answer straight away, ask "who could use that, and what for?" first.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| My favorite color | My home address | The name of my school |
| My favorite dinosaur | My phone number | A game I like to play |
| My birthday | What I had for lunch | My password |
| A photo of my house | My best friend's name | My favorite song |

**Talk about it:** the private pile is not scary, it is just yours. Ask who is allowed to know the things in that pile.

</div>

<div class="answers">

<details>
<summary>Answer key</summary>

Private: my home address, the name of my school, my phone number, my birthday, my password, a photo of my house, my best friend's name.

OK to share: my favorite color, my favorite dinosaur, a game I like to play, what I had for lunch, my favorite song.

"My best friend's name" is the one worth slowing down on. It is private because it belongs to someone else, which is a rule kids can learn early.

</details>

</div>

## Lesson 2: Ask before you tap

At this age the habit matters more than the reasoning. A kid who asks first gives you the chance to catch things they could not have spotted on their own.

Teach kids to:
- ask before downloading
- ask before replying
- ask before sharing pictures
- ask before clicking pop-ups

Answer these questions patiently every time, even the hundredth time. Asking has to stay cheap, or they will stop.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Tap or ask?

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 10 minutes · **You need:** scissors, and something red and something green

Cut out the cards and put them face down in a stack. Give your kid one green card for "I can tap this" and one red card for "I should ask first." Turn over a card, read it, and let them hold up a color. Play it fast, like a quiz show.

| | |
|---|---|
| A window says **YOU WON A PRIZE!** | The next episode of the show we picked together |
| A button says **Download now** | A game we already installed together |
| Someone I don't know sends a message | A video says "click the link below" |
| A box asks me to type a password | A friend from school sends a picture of their dog |
| A pop-up says **Your device has a virus** | The back button |

**Talk about it:** ask your kid to explain one of their red cards. Their reason matters more than the answer.

</div>

<div class="answers">

<details>
<summary>Answer key</summary>

Ask first: the prize window, the download button, the message from a stranger, the "click the link below" video, the password box, the virus pop-up.

Tap away: the next episode of the agreed show, the game you installed together, the picture from a school friend, the back button.

The virus pop-up is the one most kids get wrong. Real devices do not warn you that way, and the warning itself is the trick.

</details>

</div>

## Lesson 3: Trusted adults

Understanding who a trusted adult is can help kids identify attempts at manipulation later. Someone who wants to hurt a kid has to get between that kid and the adults around them, so a child who knows exactly who they can go to is harder to isolate.

Two phrases worth teaching word for word:

- "A trusted adult is someone my family trusts, who helps keep me safe, listens to me, and never asks me to keep unsafe secrets."
- "If something online feels confusing, upsetting, secret, or pushy, tell a grown-up."

Say the rest of this lesson to your kid directly. A trusted adult is someone who:
- Keeps you safe and wants what's best for you.
- Listens when you have a question or concern.
- Believes you when something doesn't feel right.
- Helps solve problems instead of making them worse.
- Respects your privacy and doesn't ask you to keep unsafe secrets.
- Encourages you to tell another trusted adult if they can't help.
- Tells the truth.
- Encourages asking for help.

<div class="game">

### Game: My five trusted adults

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **You need:** a pen, and crayons if you want

Have your kid trace their hand on the back of this sheet. Write one name on each finger, five people they could go to. Then fill in the table together and check each name against the rules.

Five is the number on purpose. One name is fragile, because that person will eventually be asleep, at work, or the problem.

| Finger | Who | They listen to me | They tell me the truth | They never ask me to keep an unsafe secret |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thumb | | | | |
| Pointer | | | | |
| Middle | | | | |
| Ring | | | | |
| Pinky | | | | |

**Talk about it:** if one name does not earn all three checkmarks, that is worth a calm conversation rather than an argument. Ask who else could go on that finger.

Put the finished hand somewhere your kid can see it. Revisit it once a year, because the list should change as they grow.

</div>

## Lesson 4: Online people are real people, but sometimes pretending

Keep it simple:
- "Some people online pretend to be someone else."
- "Just like costumes at Halloween, people online can pretend."
- Teach kids how when people hide behind a screen, they might feel more free to be mean or inappropriate.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Costume detective

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 10 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the cards. Each one is something a person said in a game chat. Read it out and ask your kid one question: **could this be a costume?**

The point is not to get it right. The point is that you can never tell from the words alone, which is the whole lesson.

| | |
|---|---|
| "I'm 6 too! What's your favorite color?" | "I'm the person who made this game." |
| "My mom says I can play until dinner." | "I can give you a hundred free coins." |
| "Do you go to a school near the big park?" | "I'm a kid, I promise." |
| "Don't tell anyone we talk." | "My name is Sam and I'm 7." |

**Talk about it:** ask your kid how they could find out if a costume is a costume. Walk them to the real answer, which is that they cannot, so a grown-up helps.

</div>
