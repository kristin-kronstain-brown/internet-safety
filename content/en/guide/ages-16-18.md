## Focus

This is the right age to discuss:
- Financial scams
- Phishing
- Employment scams
- Identity theft
- Data collection
- Consent
- Cybersecurity basics

Teens this age are about to hold their own accounts, their own money, and their own credit. The goal shifts from protecting them to handing over the judgment.

The games here work best if you play them as a peer rather than a parent. Take a turn, get one wrong, and say so.

## Lesson 1: Real-world risk

Explain how information gets used:
- Password reset questions
- Identity verification
- Targeted scams
- Impersonation
- Blackmail
- Reputation attacks
- Advertising profiles

Frame it without fear: "Most attacks use information people shared casually over time."

### Game: Reset my password

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** a pen

Below is everything a bank asks before it will reset an account password. Next to it is everything one person posted publicly over two years. Fill in the answers using only the posts.

| The bank asks | Your answer, from the posts alone |
|---|---|
| Mother's maiden name | |
| First pet's name | |
| City where you were born | |
| High school mascot | |
| Street you grew up on | |
| First car | |

**The posts:**

> - "Happy 60th to the best mom in the world ❤️ love you Grandma Alvarez too" (photo of three generations)
> - "RIP Pepper. 14 years. best dog ever 💔"
> - "back in Tulsa for the holidays, nothing changes lol"
> - "GO WARRIORS 🐺 senior night!!" (photo in a school jersey)
> - "last day in the old house on Maple before we move" (photo of a front porch)
> - "she's not much but she's mine 🚗" (photo of a red Civic)

Now go through the same six questions using your teen's own public accounts. Do it with them, not to them.

**Talk about it:** nobody in this example was careless. Six ordinary posts, spread over two years, each one completely reasonable on its own.

Then fix it: security answers do not have to be true. A pet's name can be a random string stored in a password manager, and that single change closes most of this.

### Phishing

Phishing is a fake message pretending to be a trusted organization.

Examples:
- Fake bank alerts
- Fake shipping texts
- Fake school emails
- Fake login pages

Teach:
- Don't click rushed links
- Verify independently, using a number or address you already had rather than one in the message
- Check sender addresses carefully

### Employment scams

Job scams target this age group specifically, because a first job search is the one time someone expects unsolicited messages about work.

Treat these as warning signs:
- Payment up front for training, equipment, or a background check
- A check to deposit, with instructions to send part of it onward
- Hiring with no interview, or an interview conducted entirely over chat
- Pay that does not match the work

## Lesson 2: Security basics

Two-factor authentication is the highest-value habit here. Explain that even if someone steals a password, they may still need a second code.

Encourage:
- Authenticator apps
- Unique passwords
- Software updates
- Recognizing suspicious login alerts

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Phish or legit?

**Players:** 2 or more · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the cards and deal them. Each player reads one aloud and calls it: **phish** or **legit**. Calling it right is worth one point. Naming the specific tell is worth two, and "it just feels off" scores nothing.

| | |
|---|---|
| "Your package is held at the depot. Confirm your address: usps-redelivery[.]info" | An email from your bank with no links, telling you to call the number on your card |
| "URGENT: unusual sign-in from Lagos. Not you? Secure your account here." | A push notification from your authenticator app you did not trigger |
| "Congrats on the internship! Deposit this check and wire $400 for your laptop." | A university email from an address ending in .edu, about a class you are enrolled in |
| "Your storage is full. Your photos will be deleted in 24 hours." | A text from a friend's number asking for a gift card, written oddly |
| "Hi, this is IT. Read me the code we just texted you." | Your streaming service asking you to sign in again after an app update |

**Talk about it:** ask which card would have got them on a bad day, running late, with 3% battery. Everyone has one, including you.

</div>

<div class="answers">

<details>
<summary>Answer key: the tells</summary>

Phish: the fake redelivery domain, the "unusual sign-in" scare with a link, the check-and-wire internship, the storage countdown, and anyone asking you to read a code aloud.

Legit, though still worth verifying independently: the bank email with no links, the .edu email about a real class, the streaming re-login after an update.

Two special cases. The authenticator push you did not trigger is not phishing, it is worse: it means somebody already has the password and is knocking. Deny it and change the password. The gift card text from a friend's number means their account is compromised, so verify by calling them.

The universal rule: a real organization never needs you to read them a code. That one covers most of the deck.

</details>

### How to teach without fear

Use curiosity instead of threats.

Avoid:
- "The internet is dangerous."
- "Everyone online is a predator."

Use:
- "Some people misuse information."
- "Good habits reduce problems."
- "You can learn to notice warning signs."

Overstating the threat is not a safe error. A teen who finds out the warnings were exaggerated discounts the accurate parts too.

## Lesson 3: Consent

Most of this guide is about what other people might do to your kid. This lesson is the other direction, and it is the one teens are most likely to get wrong without meaning any harm.

Cover:
- Sharing a photo or a screenshot of someone else, including a group chat, means asking them first
- Forwarding a private message changes who can see it, permanently
- Tagging or posting someone's location shares information that is theirs to share
- Pressuring someone for a photo is coercion, whatever the relationship is
- "They sent it to me" is not permission to send it to anyone else

Be direct about the legal side. Creating, holding, or forwarding an explicit image of someone under 18 is a serious offense in most places, and it stays serious when both people are teenagers and both agreed.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Would you forward it?

**Players:** 2 or more · **Time:** 25 minutes · **You need:** scissors

These cards have no answer key, on purpose. Draw one, say what you would actually do, and say what you would want done if it were about you. The second answer is the one to sit with.

Play this one without correcting anybody. A teen who gets argued with here will stop telling you the true answer, and the true answer is the only useful thing in the game.

| | |
|---|---|
| A friend sends you a screenshot of someone else's private message. | Someone forwards you a nude of a classmate. |
| Your group chat is roasting a photo of a kid you barely know. | A friend asks you to delete a photo of them you already posted. |
| You have a video of a friend falling over. It is genuinely funny. | Someone sends you a screenshot of your own message, in a chat you were not in. |
| A friend's partner asks you to send them a photo of your friend. | You are tagged in a post that shows where you are, right now. |
| Someone shares a classmate's home address in a chat, as a joke. | A friend tells you something, then says "don't tell anyone." |

**Talk about it:** the nude card has exactly one right answer and it is worth saying out loud. Do not forward it, do not save it, delete it, and tell an adult. Holding it is a crime, and forwarding it is a worse one.

</div>

## Lesson 4: Normalize asking for help

Kids should know:
- They will not automatically lose device privileges
- They can report mistakes safely
- Adults can help solve problems calmly

This is one of the biggest protective factors you have. It is also the one that has to be established before anything goes wrong, because it is not credible offered for the first time in a crisis.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: The 2am problem

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 25 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Draw a card. It is 2am and this has just happened. Your teen answers three questions, and then you answer the same three about your own worst version.

1. Who do you call first?
2. What are the first ten words you say?
3. What are you afraid will happen?

Question three is the real one. Answer it honestly about yourself before you ask them to.

| | |
|---|---|
| You sent a photo you immediately regretted. | You lost $300 to a trade that was never going to happen. |
| Someone is threatening to post something about you. | You clicked a link and now your account is sending messages you did not write. |
| A friend just posted something that scared you. | You said something in a group chat that got screenshotted. |
| You are being asked for money and it is getting worse. | Someone you have never met in person says they are coming to your town. |

Finish by agreeing on the actual answer to question one, with a name and a backup name. Then say the sentence out loud: **you will not lose your phone for telling me.**

If you cannot honestly say that sentence, the game has found something worth fixing, and that is a useful result too.

**Talk about it:** the fear in question three is almost always about the reaction, not the problem. That is the part you control.

</div>

## Practice real scenarios

Role-play:
- Suspicious messages
- Fake giveaways
- Requests for secrets
- Peer pressure
- Someone asking for photos
- Scam texts

Practice responses like:
- "I'm not comfortable with that."
- "I need to ask my parent."
- "I don't share personal information online."

Saying the words out loud in a low-stakes moment is what makes them available in a high-stakes one.
