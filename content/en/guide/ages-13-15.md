## Focus

This is the right age to discuss:
- Persuasion systems
- Advanced manipulation
- Reputation, permanence, and identity
- Healthy skepticism

Avoid lectures. Use collaboration. A teenager who feels lectured will argue with the framing instead of thinking about the content, and you will have taught them nothing except that the topic causes friction.

The games in this section are card decks and sorting games rather than worksheets. Play them at a table, take a side yourself, and be willing to lose the argument.

## Lesson 1: Persuasion systems

Explain how platforms influence behavior.

Teach that apps are often designed to:
- Keep attention
- Encourage sharing
- Increase emotional reactions
- Keep users scrolling

This is not evil, it is business design. Say that plainly, because a teen who thinks you are being dramatic will discount the rest of the conversation.

Teach teens to ask:
- Why is this being shown to me?
- What does this app gain if I stay longer?
- Is this trying to make me emotional?

Explain what algorithms are, and how what they collect gets used to build a picture of you.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Why am I seeing this?

**Players:** 2 or more · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the cards. Each is something that turned up in a feed. Draw one and answer three questions out loud, then the next player draws.

1. Why did the app show me this?
2. What does the app get if I stop scrolling here?
3. What does it want me to do in the next ten seconds?

| | |
|---|---|
| A video captioned "nobody is talking about this" | An ad for something you mentioned out loud yesterday |
| A creator you like, arguing with a creator you dislike | A post from someone you went to school with, doing well |
| "9 out of 10 people can't solve this" | A clip that cuts off right before the answer |
| Breaking news with no source and 40,000 shares | A comment section that is already a fight |
| A 15 second recipe you will never make | Someone's apology video |

**Talk about it:** run the same three questions on whatever is genuinely at the top of your own feed right now. Doing it to yourself in front of them works better than doing it to a card.

</div>

## Lesson 2: Advanced manipulation

### Love bombing

Love bombing is excessive affection or attention used to create fast emotional dependence.

Signs:
- Instant intense attachment
- Pressure for constant contact
- Guilt for setting boundaries

### Sextortion

Sextortion is when someone threatens to share images or information unless demands are met.

Important teaching:
- Victims are usually manipulated gradually, so there is rarely a single obvious moment to catch
- Shame keeps people silent
- Getting help early matters
- Avoid blame-focused language

Teens need the next step, not just the definition. Teach them that if it happens: stop replying, do not pay, do not send more, keep the messages, and tell an adult. Paying does not end it, and complying tends to increase the demands rather than satisfy them.

[Take It Down](https://takeitdown.ncmec.org/) can help remove images of someone who was under 18 when the image was made, and the [NCMEC CyberTipline](https://report.cybertip.org/) takes reports. Both are free.

Say out loud, before anything happens, that they will not be in trouble for telling you. That sentence is the reason a kid picks up the phone three days sooner.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: The escalation ladder

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 25 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the ten cards and shuffle them. These are messages from a single conversation, spread over about three weeks. Put them back in order, first contact at the bottom, worst at the top.

| | |
|---|---|
| "wait you play this too? i'm on most nights" | "if you don't send another one i'll post the first one" |
| "you're so much more mature than people our age" | "send me one back, it's only fair" |
| "my parents are the same, it's so unfair" | "i can't believe you'd do this to me after everything" |
| "here's a pic of me, your turn 😊" | "i'd never screenshot anything, you can trust me" |
| "don't tell anyone we talk, they'd make it weird" | "just talk to me on this other app, it's more private" |

Once the ladder is built, ask the only question that matters: **where would you have stopped?**

Then ask a harder one. **Where would it have been hardest to stop?**

**Talk about it:** end by saying plainly what happens at your house if a kid reaches rung eight. They need to hear that the answer is help, not punishment, at a moment when nothing is wrong.

</div>

<div class="answers">

<details>
<summary>Answer key: what the ordering shows</summary>

A reasonable order is: shared interest, shared complaint about parents, flattery, move to a private app, request for secrecy, a promise of trust, a photo sent to them, a photo asked for in return, guilt, then the threat.

There is no single rung where it obviously turns. Every step is small, and every step is a little harder to refuse than the one before it, because refusing now means admitting the last one was a mistake. That is the design, and naming it is the lesson.

The threat rung is where most people think the crime starts. It is not. It started at "don't tell anyone we talk."

</details>

## Lesson 3: Reputation, permanence, and identity

Discuss:
- Screenshots
- Colleges and employers
- Impersonation
- Account breaches
- AI-generated fake images and videos

Teach: "Your online identity is partly built by what you post and partly by what others can collect."

Don't let them think that one mistake ends their life. These things can be repaired, but it takes time.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: The screenshot test

**Players:** 2 or more · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the cards and sort them into three piles: **fine forever**, **fine now but not in five years**, and **never fine**.

Sort your own pile at the same time, from your life at their age. Compare afterwards. Expect to disagree, and expect to be wrong about at least one.

| | |
|---|---|
| A photo of you at a concert | A joke that is funny only if you know the person |
| A rant about a teacher, posted to 40 followers | A photo of your street sign in the background |
| A political opinion you hold strongly today | A group chat message about someone who is not in it |
| A gym progress photo | Your take on a celebrity everyone hates this week |
| A photo of a friend you did not ask first | Your username, reused on six other sites |

Then take the "never fine" pile and ask one more question about each: **who else already has a copy?**

**Talk about it:** the middle pile is the real lesson. Nothing in it is wrong today, and that is exactly the problem, because nobody deletes things they do not currently regret.

</div>

## Lesson 4: Healthy skepticism

Teach teens to verify:
- Images
- Viral claims
- "News"
- Emotional posts
- Giveaways
- Investment schemes

Ask:
- Who benefits?
- Is there evidence?
- Is this trying to provoke outrage?
- Can I verify this elsewhere?

The outrage question is the highest-yield one. Content engineered to make someone angry is the most likely to be shared and the least likely to be checked.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Who benefits?

**Players:** 2 or more · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Draw a card and answer two questions: **who benefits if I believe this**, and **who benefits if I share it**. They are not always the same person, and noticing the gap is the skill.

Score a point for naming a beneficiary the other player missed.

| | |
|---|---|
| "Doctors don't want you to know this one trick" | "This stock is about to explode, I'm only telling my followers" |
| "Share this to show you care" | "The media is refusing to cover this story" |
| "I made $8,000 last month working from my phone" | "Only 3 spots left in the program" |
| "Do your own research" | "They deleted my last video about this" |
| A charity post with no charity named | A screenshot of a tweet, with no link to the tweet |

**Talk about it:** finish with the one that is hardest to dismiss. "They deleted my last video" is unfalsifiable by design, and being unfalsifiable is itself the tell.

</div>
