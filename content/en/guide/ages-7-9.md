Kids this age start gaming, messaging, searching, and watching videos independently. That independence is the reason the lessons shift from "ask an adult first" to "here is how to tell what is happening."

## Focus

This is the right age to discuss:
- Manipulation
- Tricks
- Oversharing
- Digital permanence

## Lesson 1: Manipulation

When someone is trying to manipulate, they tend to reach for one of five tactics:

- **Kid-friendly language.** They talk the way kids talk, so they read as another kid rather than an adult.
    - "omg same, my parents are so strict too"
- **Flattery.** Saying extra nice things buys trust quickly.
    - "You're so mature."
    - "You're my favorite."
- **Urgency.** Scammers want people to act before thinking.
    - "Hurry!"
    - "You have to do this now."
- **Secrecy.** Safe relationships do not depend on hiding things from trusted adults.
    - "Keep this between us."
    - "Don't tell your parents."
- **Too good to be true.** A prize nobody earned is bait.
    - "Click here to win!"
    - "You only have one chance!"

### Game: Red flag bingo

**Players:** 2 or 3 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **You need:** one card each, and something to mark with

Give every player a **different** card. The three below are not the same, so you will not all win at once. With more than three players, reprint a card and let two people share it.

Each card has its own **Print this card** button, and printing the whole game puts each card on a separate sheet either way.

The grown-up running the game keeps the message list, which is in the answer key under this game.

1. A grown-up reads out one message, in any order.
2. Everyone says which tactic it is.
3. Mark **every** square on your card with that tactic, not just one.
4. First to four in a row wins: across, down, or corner to corner.

A round only takes about three messages, so play a few. Read them in a different order each time, because the order is what decides who wins.

**Talk about it:** after somebody wins, ask which message sounded the most normal. That is the one to remember.

## Lesson 2: Tricks

Introduce the idea of tricky behavior. The useful framing at this age is not "who is a bad person" but "what is this person trying to get from me," because a kid can answer the second question and cannot reliably answer the first.

| Some people online try to get… | Example of tricky behavior                                                  |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Attention**                  | "You're the only person who understands me. Can we chat all the time?"      |
| **Information**                | "Tell me your full name, where you go to school, and your favorite places." |
| **Money**                      | "Send me $10 and I'll send you a surprise prize."                           |
| **Pictures**                   | "Send me a picture of yourself. Don't show anyone else."                    |
| **Access to accounts**         | "I need your password to help you fix your game account."                   |

### Home Alone references

- Marv tried to trick Kevin into thinking they were Santa Claus and an elf.
- Harry said, "Be a good little fellow and open the door."

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: What do they want?

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **You need:** scissors, and five labels

Lay out five labels: **attention**, **information**, **money**, **pictures**, **account access**. Cut out the message cards and sort them under the label that says what the sender is actually after.

Some cards fit two labels. Those are the interesting ones, so let your kid argue for both.

| | |
|---|---|
| "What's your gamer tag? I'll add you." | "My account got banned, can I borrow yours for one match?" |
| "Nobody else gets me like you do." | "Send a selfie so I know you're real." |
| "Do you take the bus or does your mom drive you?" | "I'll trade you a legendary skin, just send the gift card code first." |
| "Are you home by yourself right now?" | "Why didn't you answer me? I waited all night." |
| "What's your school mascot? Mine's a tiger." | "Just tell me your password, I can fix it faster than support can." |

**Talk about it:** point out that none of these cards asks for anything scary. Every one of them is a small, reasonable-sounding question, which is exactly how it works.

## Lesson 3: Oversharing

Password safety is the concrete version of this lesson, and it is the one kids this age can act on.

Teach:
- Long passwords are stronger
- Don't reuse passwords
- Don't share passwords or device codes with friends

Connect it back to oversharing. Someone who knows your pet's name, your birthday, and your favorite team is partway to guessing your password or answering your reset questions.

Good rule: "Passwords are like toothbrushes: personal and not for sharing."

<div class="game">

### Game: Password detective

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **You need:** a pen

Here is everything a kid named Riley has posted publicly. Read it with your kid, then work out together which of the four passwords at the bottom is most likely Riley's.

> **Riley, age 9**
>
> - Posted a photo of a beagle. Caption: "Biscuit turned 3 today!!"
> - Posted a drawing of a soccer ball with the number 7 on it.
> - Commented: "my birthday is november 12 so im the youngest in my class"
> - Posted a photo in a blue jersey that says **Westfield Tigers**
> - Bio: "beagles > everything"

Which one is Riley's password?

1. `Xk49#pqLm2`
2. `Biscuit2016`
3. `correct-horse-battery`
4. `9182736455`

Now play it in reverse. Ask your kid to invent a password that none of Riley's posts would help you guess, then check it against the list: is it long, is it unrelated to anything Riley shared, and is it used anywhere else?

**Talk about it:** the point is not that Riley was careless. It is that five ordinary posts were enough.

## Lesson 4: Digital permanence

Explain what a digital footprint is and how it can be used against them.

Anything shared online may:
- Be copied
- Be screenshotted
- Spread to other people

Deleting something removes your copy, not everyone else's. That distinction is the whole lesson.

Good phrasing: "The internet remembers more than people expect."

<div class="game">

### Game: The copy machine

**Players:** 3 or more, so it works best at a table with the whole family · **Time:** 10 minutes · **You need:** a pen each

Your kid draws a small picture in box 1. Fold the sheet so only box 1 shows, pass it on, and the next person copies the drawing into box 2. Keep going until the boxes run out.

| | | |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **2** | **3** |
| | | |
| **4** | **5** | **6** |
| | | |

Now hand the sheet back to your kid and ask them to delete the drawing. They can scribble out box 1 as hard as they like.

Boxes 2 through 6 are still there. That is the lesson, and no explanation gets it across as fast as watching it happen.

**Talk about it:** ask who has a copy now. Then ask a harder one: if box 4 got passed to somebody outside the room, could your kid get it back?

</div>

## Build community in real life

This section is for you rather than your kid. A child with somewhere to belong offline is a much harder target, because the offer of friendship stops being scarce.

- Where are your kids finding togetherness and community? If the answer is only online, that is worth addressing directly.
- What are the adults around your kids modeling as appropriate?
- Help define what a healthy friendship looks like, in concrete terms.
- Teach what "care" looks like before a predator gets to define it for them.
