Preteens begin social apps, group chats, fandoms, gaming communities, and independent browsing. They are also old enough to understand motive, which is what makes this the right age to teach how manipulation actually works rather than just what to avoid.

## Focus

This is the right age to discuss:
- Catfishing
- Scams
- Social engineering
- Emotional manipulation
- AI
- Privacy habits that work in any app

## Lesson 1: Catfishing

Catfishing is when someone pretends to be a different person online to gain trust, attention, friendship, money, pictures, or emotional control.

Examples:
- Pretending to be another kid
- Using stolen photos
- Lying about age or identity

Teach: "You cannot fully verify someone just because they seem nice."

Being nice is the cheapest thing a person can fake, which is exactly why it is the first thing they fake.

<div class="game">

### Game: Profile detective

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** a pen

Two people added your kid this week. Read both profiles together and work out which tells you something real and which tells you nothing.

| | **Account A** | **Account B** |
|---|---|---|
| Name | jordan_m_04 | xX_ShadowKing_Xx |
| Photo | A kid at a swim meet, slightly blurry | A professional-looking photo of a teenager |
| Joined | Two years ago | Three weeks ago |
| Posts | 60 posts over two years, mostly swim meets and a dog | 4 posts, all reposted memes |
| Followers | 80, mostly from the same school | 1,200 followers, following 4,000 |
| Bio | "west middle. 400 free. biscuit's human" | "just here to make friends :)" |
| First message | "hey are you on the summer league team too" | "hey you seem really cool, how old are you?" |

Ask your kid to mark every line where Account B looks off, then answer one question: **what could Account B fake if it wanted to look like Account A?**

**Talk about it:** ask your kid whether Account A being real would make it okay to share their school. It would not, and that is worth separating out.

</div>

<div class="answers">

<details>
<summary>Answer key: what to steer toward</summary>

The tells on Account B are the new account, the tiny post history, the follower ratio, the generic bio, and a first message that asks a personal question before anything else.

The real answer to the last question is: all of it. Every single line can be faked with enough effort, including a two-year history. That is why the conclusion is never "this one is safe," it is "I cannot tell from a profile, so it does not get treated as a friend."

</details>

</div>

## Lesson 2: Scams

A scam is a trick designed to get:
- Money
- Passwords
- Account access
- Personal information

At this age scams usually arrive dressed as something a kid already wants, such as free game currency, a giveaway, or a rare item trade. Teach them to treat an unexpected offer as an unfinished sentence: someone is getting something out of this, and it is worth asking what.

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Too good to be true

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 15 minutes · **You need:** scissors

Cut out the cards and sort them into three piles: **real**, **scam**, and **cannot tell yet**. The third pile is the important one, and a kid who never uses it is guessing rather than thinking.

| | |
|---|---|
| A game you own announces a free seasonal update in the app itself. | A stranger offers to gift you a battle pass if you join their server. |
| A creator you follow runs a giveaway you enter by liking the video. | A DM says you won a giveaway you never entered. |
| A site offers 50,000 free coins if you enter your username and password. | A friend you know in person offers to trade you an item in game. |
| A message says your account will be deleted in 24 hours unless you verify. | The game's own store has a skin on sale for real money. |
| Someone offers to buy your account for $200, paid after you hand it over. | A pop-up says you are the 1,000,000th visitor. |

**Talk about it:** for every card in the scam pile, ask the same question. Who ends up with money, and who ends up with an account?

</div>

## Lesson 3: Social engineering

Social engineering is a manipulation technique where someone uses psychology instead of hacking.

Examples:
- Pretending to be support staff
- Pretending to be a friend
- Creating panic
- Guilt-tripping
- Pretending there's an emergency

Teach: "Many scams succeed because people are emotional, rushed, or trusting."

<div class="game game-cards">

### Game: Who are you really?

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** scissors

You play the person sending the message, reading a card out loud and staying in character. Your kid gets exactly one move: ask a single question that would prove or disprove who you say you are.

Then they decide what to do. There are only ever three answers, and the third is usually right: reply, ignore, or check through a different channel.

| | |
|---|---|
| "Hi, I'm from the game's support team. We've detected a problem with your account." | "It's Grandma, I'm on my friend's phone, mine broke. Can you send me your mom's number?" |
| "This is your school's IT department. We need your login to fix your Chromebook." | "Hey it's me, Aidan, I got locked out of my old account so this is my new one." |
| "Your mom asked me to pick you up today. She's running late." | "I'm a moderator. Someone reported you, and I need to check your messages." |
| "You've been chosen as a beta tester. I just need your email and password to set it up." | "Your package couldn't be delivered. Confirm your address here." |

**Talk about it:** every one of these is answered the same way, by hanging up and contacting the real person or company using a number or app you already had. Say that out loud until it is boring.

</div>

## Lesson 4: Emotional manipulation

Kids this age need scripts they can recognize. Someone might use misinformation or threaten your kid's reputation to appeal to their emotions and get what they want.

### Common manipulation patterns

- Guilt: "If you were really my friend…"
- Isolation: "Nobody understands you except me."
- Pressure: "Send it now."
- Fake emergencies: "Your account will be deleted!"
- Fear of missing out: "Everyone else already did it."

Teach kids to pause when emotions suddenly spike. The spike is not incidental to the manipulation, it is the mechanism, because a person who is panicking does not stop to check anything.

Good rule: "Strong emotions are a signal to slow down."

<div class="game">

### Game: The feelings thermometer

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** a pen

Read each message out loud. Your kid marks how it would make them feel on a scale of 1 to 5, where 1 is calm and 5 is a racing heart. Then together, name which pattern it is using.

| Message | 1 · 2 · 3 · 4 · 5 | Which pattern? |
|---|---|---|
| "If you were actually my friend you'd do this." | | |
| "Everyone in the group chat already sent theirs." | | |
| "Your account will be permanently deleted in 1 hour." | | |
| "Nobody else gets you. They'd all judge you." | | |
| "Send it now, I have to go in a minute." | | |
| "I told you something private, so now you owe me." | | |
| "I guess I'll just tell everyone you ignored me." | | |

Now go back and circle every message your kid scored a 4 or a 5.

**Talk about it:** the circled ones are not the dangerous messages, they are the ones that work. Teach the rule that a 4 or a 5 means put the phone down for ten minutes, because no real emergency gets worse from ten minutes.

</div>

## Lesson 5: AI

Kids this age meet AI as a homework tool and a toy, and rarely as something that can be aimed at them. Cover both directions: AI can make things up confidently, and AI can be used to fake a voice, a face, or a photo convincingly enough to fool someone who knows the real person.

- [Google AI literacy guide](https://services.google.com/fh/files/misc/bia_ai-literacy-guide_en.pdf)

<div class="game">

### Game: Could this be faked?

**Players:** 2 · **Time:** 20 minutes · **You need:** a pen

Do not play the "spot the AI picture" game. Your kid will beat you at it today and lose to a better model next year, and the lesson they take away will be the wrong one.

Play this instead. For each item, decide together how hard it would be to fake right now, then answer the column that actually matters.

| Thing | Easy, hard, or impossible to fake? | How would I check? |
|---|---|---|
| A text from a friend's number | | |
| A voice note that sounds like your cousin | | |
| A photo of a celebrity holding a sign | | |
| A video of a news anchor reading a story | | |
| A photo of a classmate that never existed | | |
| A video call with someone's face and voice | | |
| Your mom walking into the room | | |

**Talk about it:** the right-hand column should end up saying roughly the same thing every time, which is to check through a channel the faker does not control. The last row is the only one that cannot be faked, and it is worth ending on.

Agree on a family code word while you are here. If anyone calls in a panic asking for money or a ride, the code word settles it.

</div>

## Privacy habits that work in any app

These habits do not depend on which app is popular this year, which is what makes them worth teaching as habits rather than as settings.

Teach kids to:
- Keep accounts private when possible
- Disable location sharing unless it is needed
- Avoid posting live locations
- Use nicknames instead of full names
- Avoid showing school logos, addresses, and schedules
- Think before posting photos of bedrooms, cars, or neighborhoods
- Decline friend requests from unknown people
- Avoid quizzes that collect personal information
- Use parent-approved password managers

Explain why: small details get combined into a bigger profile of a person. No single post gives anything away, which is exactly why kids underestimate the total.

For example, a school shirt plus a first name plus a photo at a soccer field is enough for someone to identify a child.
