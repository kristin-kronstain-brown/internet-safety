There are always going to be new apps that kids want to install. Form a plan for deciding which apps are OK and which are not, so the answer does not come down to how worn down you are on the day they ask. Apple recommends installing from the Kids category, but kids don't always find things that way.

## Preparing a device

Before you start installing apps, lay the foundation for the future. Even if you are just giving your 3 year old a tablet to watch in the car, take these steps early. That way, when they're 8, they will be used to the process.

1. Set up the device using a family Apple or Gmail account. Use a generic name and a random photo, so the account itself gives nothing away.

    Expect to hit a limit here. Many games tie progress to a single login, so siblings sharing one account cannot each have their own save.

1. Install a parent monitoring app.

1. Learn which settings you actually control in that app. Find this out before you need it, not during an argument.

## To install or not to install?

Go through these questions and decide what you are OK with at your child's current age. Repeat the process and adjust your answers as the kids get older.

### What is the age rating?

Ratings might not line up with what you care about as a parent. A game can be rated **Everyone** or **4+** and still have an open chat. It is still a place to start. If an app is rated for teens, that can be a quick no for an 8 year old.

Learn more about the age-based ratings for your operating system:

- [Google Play](https://support.google.com/googleplay/answer/6209544)
    - On any app's details page, in the Information section, look for tags and labels alongside the age rating.
- [Apple](https://developer.apple.com/help/app-store-connect/reference/app-information/age-ratings-values-and-definitions/)
    - On any app's details page, in the Information section, look for the "Age rating" and "Contains" sections.

### What can other users say to my kid?

For most families this question matters more than the age rating does, because it decides whether strangers can reach your child at all. Ratings rarely answer it directly, so look for the specific capability.

Treat these as a no until your kid has been taught what predatory behavior looks like:

- Open chat with strangers, including lobby, guild, and party chat
- Direct or group messaging
- Voice or video chat
- Public posting
- Disappearing or secret messages
- Trading, gifting, or sending resources between users

Treat these as a maybe, depending on the app and the kid:

- Chat restricted to an approved friends list
- User-generated content that your kid can see but not respond to

Treat these as the safest options:

- Chat that your parent monitoring app can disable
- Local-only chat over shared WiFi or Bluetooth
- Preset phrase chat, where users pick from a fixed list rather than typing freely

Profanity filters are worth having, but do not count them as protection. They block words, not intent, and a filter has never stopped anyone from asking a child where they go to school.

### Could the permissions be used to make the app better?

Apps legitimately need permissions. Some of what an app collects goes toward making the software work better, rather than toward collecting information about your kid personally. The question is whether a given permission makes sense for what this particular app does.

#### Generally reasonable

These permissions can genuinely improve an app and are not suspicious by themselves:

- Internet access
- Notifications
- Storage or photo access limited to saving the app's own output
- Approximate location
- Name, profile picture, email address
- Device manufacturer information

#### Requires stronger justification

These are not automatically bad, but you should be able to name the feature that needs them:

- Camera
- Microphone
- Full photo library access
- Contacts
- Calendar
- Bluetooth
- Health and fitness data
- SMS access
- Call logs
- Accessibility services

Anything on this list turns into a red flag when the app has no plausible use for it. A video calling app needs the camera. A coloring app does not need your contacts.

#### Potential red flags

For a game or a simple app, ask hard questions about any of these:

- Precise location
- Continuous background location
- Permission to install other apps
- An in-app browser instead of the system default browser, which can route around parental monitoring
- Broad file-system access
- Device administrator privileges

### Get AI to tell you

Understanding what an app actually does is hard from the labels and description on its details page. It is often easier to write down the functionality you care about, sort it into categories, then have AI tell you how the app measures up.

Here's an example. In this case the parent does not want the kids interacting with other users until they have been through training on predatory behavior.

Check the answer against the app's own store listing before you act on it. AI tools are confidently wrong about app features fairly often, especially for smaller games and for features added in recent updates.

```text
I am designing the parameters of a parental review for an app my child wants to install. Look for the following functionality and tell me which things this app has.

Red:
- Precise location shared with other users
- Multiplayer / Competitive multiplayer
- Messaging
- Users interact
- Open chats
- Direct or group messaging
- Allows public posting
- Live messaging in voice or video chats
- Secret or disappearing messages, like in Snapchat or secret messaging in Facebook marketplace
- Users can trade or give each other resources
- Unrestricted web access, which might have embedded browser functionality or a browser app to circumvent parental monitoring

Yellow:
- Precise location shared with the app developer
- Approximate location shared with other users
- Approximate location shared with the app developer
- Shares user generated content such as broadly distributed videos, photos, text, and/or audio created by users of the app

Green:
- Chats that can be disabled in a parent monitoring app
- Chats that are local only through sharing a WiFi or Bluetooth connection
- Chats include profanity filters

Describe the app's capabilities around these areas:
- Moderation tools in open chats
- How are ads used to impact user play?
- What is the age rating?
- Is there any other functionality a parent should know about?
```

## Open questions

These are the parts of the process this guide has not solved yet.

**How do you find out when an app changes?** An approved app can add chat, trading, or a built-in browser in any update, and the store will not tell you. Release notes usually say "bug fixes." Turning off automatic updates at least means changes arrive when you choose, but it leaves security patches unapplied, which is its own problem. For now the honest answer is to re-run the review periodically on the apps your kid uses most.

**Is the app the problem, or the users?** An app can collect very little and still be dangerous because of who is on it, and an app can collect a lot and pose no realistic risk to your kid. The data security section of a store listing answers the first question and says nothing about the second, so it is worth less than its prominence suggests.
